We had 3,000 ETL pipelines on an in-house orchestration platform, and the platform needed to move to a new version. Each pipeline fed something: a report, a downstream job, a reconciliation, a number someone looked at on Monday morning. None of their owners had asked for the upgrade.

The platform change was hard. Keeping the people behind those pipelines confident while their workloads changed underneath them was harder.

## What 3,000 pipelines means

The count undersells it. Each pipeline has upstream sources, downstream consumers, a schedule that someone depends on, and an expectation about when the data lands and what happens when it doesn't. Many of those expectations were never written down. They lived with the teams consuming the data, and we found them when a load ran late or a number moved.

So the upgrade was less a deployment than a change program across every team that touched the data.

## What changed between versions

An in-house platform has no vendor migration guide. The differences between versions were whatever our platform had changed, and pipelines built on the old version carried assumptions nobody had recorded. These are the areas we had to check:

| Category | What can change between versions |
|---|---|
| Compatibility | Pipeline definitions or configuration that the new version parses or validates differently |
| Scheduling and dependencies | How triggers, ordering, and cross-pipeline dependencies resolve |
| Runtime behaviour | Execution environment, resource limits, performance characteristics |
| Failure handling | Retries, timeouts, partial failures, and the state left behind |
| Data correctness | Whether the same inputs still produce the same outputs |
| Observability | Whether existing alerts, logs, and runbooks still point at the right signals |

Most pipelines hit none of these. Some hit several at once, and they were rarely the ones we expected.

## Moving in waves

A single cutover would have put every unknown into production on the same night. If something failed, we'd have had no way to separate a platform defect from a pipeline-specific quirk, and no earlier results to reason from. It would also have asked every owner to accept the full risk on day one with nothing to base that on.

We migrated progressively instead: move a group, validate it, learn from it, fix the process, then move the next, slightly riskier group.

```text
lower-risk workloads
  -> validate -> learn -> improve the migration process
  -> next group, slightly higher risk
  -> validate -> learn -> improve the migration process
  -> ...
  -> most critical workloads
```

Every wave gave us evidence that the new platform behaved, and a migration process a little better than the one before it.

## Choosing the order

The order turned out to be one of our main risk controls. We placed each pipeline using six factors:

- **Criticality**: what breaks for the business if it's late or wrong.
- **Dependencies**: how many pipelines and consumers sit downstream.
- **Complexity**: how much of the platform's behaviour it relies on.
- **Testability**: whether we could check correctness with confidence before production.
- **Operational maturity**: whether the owning team could support and diagnose it.
- **Known migration risk**: whether it used features we knew differed between versions.

Early waves were low on criticality and high on testability, so they were cheap to get wrong and easy to prove right. That made them the best place to find out what our process couldn't handle yet. Dependencies then constrained everything else, because you can't move a pipeline without knowing what feeds it and what it feeds, and the waves ended up forming around dependency boundaries as much as around risk.

The most critical pipelines went last. That can look like avoidance. By the time those pipelines moved, the earlier waves had already surfaced the compatibility issues, exposed the gaps in our testing, and made the new platform's failure modes familiar. The critical workloads still carried the most risk. What had changed was how much we knew about it.

## A question for each environment

Promotion through environments is often a formality. We gave each environment one question to answer, and a pipeline didn't advance until it had the answer.

```text
development      integration / test       pre-production           production
-----------      ------------------       --------------           ----------
Does it run?     Is the output correct?   Does it behave under     Can it be operated
                 Do dependencies still    realistic conditions?    and supported?
                 resolve?                 Are monitoring and
                                          performance acceptable?
```

Your environment names will differ. The order is the point: cheap questions first, where failure costs little, and expensive ones last, with the most evidence behind them. A pipeline running cleanly in development had proven it parses and executes, and nothing more.

It also changes what a defect means. Finding one in a lower environment is the process doing its job.

## Evidence that other people can see

Testing did two jobs. It validated the technology, and it gave owners something concrete to decide on. The second job only works if the results leave the migration team, so we published them:

```text
test -> observe -> find defects -> improve the migration approach
     -> record the evidence -> share it
     -> earn agreement for the next, riskier group
```

"Stakeholders" was too coarse a word to plan with. Each group was exposed to a different failure and needed a different kind of evidence:

| Stakeholder | The question they are asking | Evidence that answers it |
|---|---|---|
| Engineering | Will the platform and the pipelines work? | Execution results, defect trends, compatibility findings |
| Data and application owners | Will the data stay correct? | Output validation against pre-migration behaviour |
| Operations | Can we support this after cutover? | Working monitoring, current runbooks, a clean handover |
| Business stakeholders | Will our critical processes keep running? | A track record on comparable workloads, a recovery plan |
| Leadership | Is risk going down, and are we on track? | Waves completed, defects closed, what remains and why |

A defect count means something to an engineer. A data owner wants to know which of their pipelines are affected and what that does to the data they rely on, so we reported in those terms.

The tension we kept running into was predictable. We'd decide something was ready because we were close to the evidence, and the people who owned the risk hadn't seen enough to agree. Both positions were reasonable. Pushing past that with authority would have cost more trust than it saved time, so we closed the gap with more of the same: repeated clean waves, open reporting of what went wrong, a named owner for each residual risk, and progression criteria agreed before the go/no-go meeting. Once the criteria were agreed in advance, the meeting became a check against them and stopped being a negotiation.

## Leading it without owning it

I had almost no formal authority over any of this. The pipelines belonged to other teams, and the risk sat with their owners. What moved things forward was structure people could push back on: a sequencing model they could challenge, criteria they had signed up to, and evidence that made each decision a little easier than the last.

Day to day, my job was working out what had changed between platform versions, where those changes put pipelines at risk, coordinating testing across teams and environments, chasing issues and exceptions through to resolution, and feeding each lesson into the next wave.

## Before your first wave

If you're facing one of these, the work that pays off most happens before anything moves. Map the dependency graph before you build the pipeline list, since what feeds what sets the order you can migrate in. Write down the question each environment has to answer; an environment without one tells you nothing when a workload passes through it. Pick a first wave for what it will teach you, and expect it to expose gaps.

Then agree the progression criteria with the owners while nobody is under pressure. Criteria written the night before a cutover turn into a negotiation. And keep the critical workloads back until the process has a track record. The upgrade is finished when the organisation can move its most important pipelines with confidence, which is well after the day the new version goes live.
